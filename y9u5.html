Pause before believing or sharing.

Revision notes · All six lessons

What you need to know

These notes follow your Year 9 AI scheme of work. The classroom end-of-unit task is intentionally not reproduced here; these pages build the knowledge and reasoning skills needed for it.

Lesson 1 · AI compared with traditional programming

AI describes systems that can perform tasks such as identifying patterns, making predictions, generating content or supporting decisions. A physical robot is not necessary.

Rule-based programming uses instructions chosen directly by a programmer. For example, a light might turn on when a sensor detects that a room is dark.

Machine learning creates a model from examples. For example, a trained system may learn patterns in photographs to help categorise unfamiliar images. Products can combine both approaches.

Useful question: what part of the system learns from data, and which decisions use fixed instructions?

Lesson 2 · Training data, models and tests
  1. Collect and label: gather varied examples with accurate labels.
  2. Train: the model learns statistical patterns from those examples.
  3. Test: check performance on new examples, including difficult cases.
  4. Improve: look at errors, data gaps and possible overfitting.

A false positive means incorrectly saying something belongs to a class; a false negative means failing to recognise it when it does.

More data does not fix unreliable labels or missing groups. Use suitable variety and separate testing. The original lesson explores Google Teachable Machine; the built-in practice is a simplified simulation, not a replacement for a trained image classifier.

Lesson 3 · Benefits, risks and case studies

AI may assist with examining large datasets, detecting patterns, personalising services and planning resources. Possible applications include healthcare, transport, entertainment, social media, finance and education.

Always distinguish a useful prediction from a final decision. In decisions affecting people, evaluate privacy, mistakes, fairness, appeal routes and who has the last word.

Build a balanced evaluation with four questions: What does it do? Who benefits? Who could be harmed? What evidence and safeguards would you need?

Lesson 4 · Four sources of bias and six principles

Four possible sources of bias

  1. Biased data: examples reflect unfair past decisions.
  2. Underrepresentation: some users or conditions scarcely appear in training data.
  3. Proxy variables: seemingly neutral features indirectly reflect other characteristics.
  4. Feedback loops: predictions influence later data, reinforcing the same patterns.

Six AI ethics principles

Fairness: avoid unjustified disadvantage. Transparency: explain AI use and limitations. Accountability: make responsibility clear. Privacy: safeguard personal data. Safety: prevent avoidable harm. Inclusivity: design for diverse users.

A good ethical argument uses evidence, acknowledges uncertainty and proposes realistic oversight or appeal options. The scheme’s ARIA courtroom activity is not repeated in this revision hub.

Lesson 5 · LLMs, hallucinations, deepfakes and SIFT

A large language model (LLM) learns statistical patterns in text and generates outputs by predicting tokens based on context. Human feedback can help refine its behaviour, but fluent language alone does not verify a claim.

Hallucination: an AI output presents invented or inaccurate content as if it were true. Deepfake: synthetic or altered media resembling an actual person or event. Appearance alone cannot reliably establish authenticity.

SIFT: Stop; Investigate the source; Find better coverage; Trace claims to the original context.

In schoolwork: follow school rules, verify claims, cite original information where required, and do not present generated work as independent work when it is not.

Lesson 6 · Work, AI regulation and oversight

AI can automate parts of jobs, change how professionals work and create demand for different skills. Avoid claiming a named occupation will definitely disappear. Think about tasks, not only job titles.

The EU AI Act applies a risk-based framework to AI. Different provisions apply on different dates. Some harmful practices are prohibited, while other systems have obligations linked to risk and transparency. In the UK, AI is largely overseen through existing sector-specific law and regulators rather than one general AI law.

Reasons to regulate: safety, rights, accountability and public trust. Concerns: implementation costs and slowing useful innovation. Balanced reasoning considers both.

Common misconceptions · Check yourself
  • “AI is always a robot.” Many systems are software without any robot.
  • “AI is always objective.” Data and design choices can produce biased results.
  • “Accuracy proves fairness.” Overall accuracy may hide poor results for some groups.
  • “A convincing answer must be true.” Hallucinations can sound plausible.
  • “Deepfakes are easy to spot.” Visual appearance is an unreliable test.
  • “One law fixes all AI risks.” Rules require enforcement, expertise and adaptation.
