Lesson 1
1. Cells, addresses and formatting
A spreadsheet is made of columns (letters) and rows (numbers). A cell reference identifies one cell, such as D5. A range identifies several cells, such as B3:B8, where the colon means 'to'.
TermMeaning
CellOne box at the intersection of a row and column
Cell referenceA column letter plus a row number, such as C7
RangeA selection of cells, e.g. C2:C6
Fill handleTool for copying formulas or continuing patterns
Make spreadsheets readable using clear headings, currency formatting, sensible decimal places, appropriate shading and borders. Formatting is about clarity, not just decoration.

 
Lessons 1 & 2
2. Formulas and arithmetic
Formulas tell a spreadsheet to calculate a result. They normally begin with =. A spreadsheet uses * to multiply and / to divide.
=B3+C3        add two cells
=B3-C3        subtract
=B3*C3        multiply
=B3/C3        divide
=(B3+C3)*2    brackets first
Cell references are important because a model updates automatically as the input values change. The formula =B3*C3 recalculates after a change to B3 or C3.
Common mistake: B3+C3 without the equals sign may be treated as text. Use =B3+C3.

 
Lessons 1 & 4
3. Functions and the fill handle
FunctionPurposeExample
SUMAdd all numbers in a range=SUM(C3:C9)
MINFind the smallest number=MIN(C3:C9)
MAXFind the largest number=MAX(C3:C9)
AVERAGEFind the mean=AVERAGE(C3:C9)
IFChoose a result based on a condition=IF(B3>10,"High","Low")
The fill handle can copy a rule down a column. If you copy =B3*C3 down one row, the usual relative references become =B4*C4.
For a more advanced challenge, absolute references like $B$2 remain fixed when copied.

 
Lesson 2
4. Working out revenue, costs and profit
Revenue
Money received from sales: selling price × number sold.
=B4*C4
Profit or loss
Revenue − costs. A negative answer means a loss.
=D4-E4
New example: A school art activity receives £160 and has costs of £65. Its profit is £95. If costs rise to £180 with the same revenue, it makes a £20 loss.
A useful spreadsheet model keeps revenue, costs and profit visible in separate columns.

 
Lesson 3
5. Labels, variables and rules
Label
Words that describe data, such as Price (£) or Attendance.
Variable
An input that can change, such as a cost or number of tickets.
Rule
A formula or function that calculates a result from input data.
What-if model
Change an input, predict the effect and compare the new output.
Changing a variable can affect several cells because formulas depend on other values. A model is only as good as its data and assumptions, so it should be tested.

 
Lesson 5
6. Choose and label the right chart
ChartBest forExample
BarComparing separate categoriesPupils in different clubs
LineChange over time or ordered pointsReading minutes across a week
PieParts of one wholeHow a class budget is divided
Include a clear chart title, units and axis labels where needed. Check that the scale is sensible and avoid relying only on colour. Similar-sized pie slices may be hard to compare accurately.

 
Lessons 3 & 4
7. Printing a spreadsheet clearly
Portrait pages are taller than they are wide; landscape pages are wider. Choose the one that suits your data. Use Print Preview to check page breaks, text size and margins. Fit to one page can help, but make sure the result is still readable.
Print with gridlines if requested, add an identifying header, and check whether normal view (results) or formula view (actual calculations) is required. Sometimes both are useful as evidence.

 
Across the unit
8. Predict, test and improve
Good spreadsheet work involves explaining your model, not just entering formulas. Ask: What does the answer mean? Does it make sense? What would happen if one input changed? Have I selected the correct range?
Check: values, formula references, currency formatting, chart titles, print settings and whether any assumptions might be unrealistic.
Exam tip: When asked to explain a change, state which variable changes, which formula depends on it and how the output changes. Do not just write 'it will go up'.
