Everything you need to know

Follow the same three-lesson structure as your Year 8 E-Safety scheme of work. The examples are new and focus on understanding and safe decisions.

Lesson 1 · Email: uses, strengths, weaknesses and dangers

Email lets people send messages and files electronically. Schools may send reminders, businesses may contact customers and families may share information.

Advantages

  • Fast delivery and easy to contact groups
  • Useful written record
  • Can attach useful files
  • Often costs less than printed post

Disadvantages & risks

  • Messages can be missed or sent to the wrong person
  • Unwanted mail and spam
  • Fraud, phishing and harmful attachments
  • Not ideal for every urgent or sensitive conversation

Phishing: common warning signs

  • Unexpected requests for passwords or payment
  • Pressure to act immediately or keep it secret
  • An unfamiliar or misleading sender address
  • Links to a website you do not recognise
  • An attachment you were not expecting

Remember: a message can look polished, include correct personal details and still be fraudulent. When unsure, use the usual app or website rather than following the link.

Practice: A “subscription expired” message asks for your school password. Explain what makes it suspicious and how you could verify it safely.

Lesson 2 · Real websites, malware, pharming and ransomware

Genuine websites can be imitated. The key clues include the actual domain, whether the request makes sense and whether you independently trust the service. HTTPS means the connection is encrypted, not that the website operator is trustworthy.

ThreatWhat it meansTypical harm
PhishingA message designed to trick youStolen login information or money
PharmingMisleading redirection to a false websiteEntering details on a fake page
MalwareAny software created for harmful purposesDisruption, spying or stolen data
RansomwareMalware that blocks access and demands paymentLocked files, service outages and data exposure

Types of malware

  • Virus: can attach to a file or programme and spread when executed.
  • Worm: can spread or copy itself across systems.
  • Trojan: appears legitimate but contains harmful functions.
  • Spyware: secretly collects information.
  • Ransomware: blocks access or threatens to publish stolen data to demand money.

Possible warning signs include unexpected pop-ups, changed settings, files becoming unavailable or unusual slowdowns. None of these symptoms proves malware by itself, and some infections have no visible symptoms.

How to reduce risk

  • Keep devices, browsers, apps and routers updated.
  • Install software from trusted sources only.
  • Use unique passwords and two-step verification.
  • Use security software and take notice of genuine warnings.
  • Keep protected backups of important files.
  • Tell an adult or IT staff promptly if something looks wrong.

Lesson 3 · Hacking, cybercrime and prevention

Hacking involves using technical skills to investigate or change computers or networks. Some people test security with permission. Accessing a system without authorisation can be an offence under the Computer Misuse Act 1990.

People may attempt unauthorised access to steal information, impersonate others, demand money, damage a system or interrupt a service. An ordinary family, student or business can be targeted.

Logic bombs and social engineering

  • A logic bomb is harmful code that runs when a certain condition is met.
  • Social engineering means tricking people into weakening security, for example by revealing login details.
  • Guessing or stealing passwords can allow access to someone else’s account.

Good responses to problems

  • Suspicious email: do not click; verify independently; use the NCSC reporting route.
  • Possible malware on a school device: stop using it for sensitive tasks and tell school staff or IT support.
  • Hacked account: seek adult help, recover through the genuine service and change the password.
  • Online abuse: tell a trusted adult and use the appropriate platform or specialist reporting service.
Important: Do not investigate harmful or illegal material by downloading or sharing it. Ask a trusted adult for help.

You're not on your own

Get help with online safety

If something online is worrying you, tell a trusted adult, parent, carer, teacher or the school's safeguarding staff. You do not have to solve it yourself.

If someone is in immediate danger: call 999. For a school account or device problem, tell a teacher or school IT team promptly.
