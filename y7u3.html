Everything to know for Unit 3

These notes follow your three lessons: recognising unsafe contact, protecting images and accounts, and understanding consequences. Key terms are highlighted and short examples help you apply what you know.

Lesson 1 · Making good decisions

1. Online friends and online identities

Online communities can be positive. They allow us to find people with shared interests, learn new skills and play together. The risk is that you cannot always verify who someone is or what they want.

✅ Positive opportunities

Collaborating in games, sharing ideas, enjoying hobbies and learning with friends.

⚠️ Risks to think about

False identities, requests for personal details, pressure to move into private chats or requests to meet.

Remember: An attractive profile, lots of followers, shared hobbies or a kind-sounding message do not prove someone's identity.
Lesson 1 · Recognising manipulation

2. Grooming and warning signs

Grooming is when someone builds trust or a relationship with a child in order to manipulate or exploit them. Grooming can take place online or in person, and can involve someone the child already knows.

🚩 Possible warning signs

Pressure to keep secrets, gifts with strings attached, being asked to move platforms, emotional blackmail, sexual requests or threats.

🛡️ Protective actions

Recognise that you can say no, stop responding, use block/report tools and tell a trusted adult. You never owe someone a private photo or a secret.

Not every friendly online contact is dangerous. Look for a pattern of pressure, secrecy, control or unsafe requests rather than judging by how friendly someone appears.

If a conversation worries you: You can stop communicating. Speak to a parent, carer, teacher or school safeguarding adult. For suspected online sexual abuse or grooming, CEOP can take a report.
Lesson 2 · Respect and boundaries

3. Sexting, intimate images and messages

Sexting commonly refers to sending or sharing sexual messages, images or videos using digital technology. An intimate image might be saved, copied or forwarded even when an app claims it will disappear.

Possible consequenceWhy it matters
Loss of controlSomeone else could copy or share the material beyond the original chat.
Bullying or blackmailA person might use the image or message to humiliate, threaten or pressure someone.
Digital footprintCopies may remain even after an original message is deleted.
Legal and safeguarding concernsMaking, possessing or sharing sexual images of children can be against UK law, and specialist support may be needed.

Child sexual abuse material (CSAM) is the appropriate term for sexual imagery of children. The phrase in some older learning materials, ‘child pornography’, is no longer preferred because it does not properly reflect that children are being abused or exploited.

If somebody shares or threatens to share an intimate image: Do not send more images or money, do not forward the image, and tell a trusted adult. Childline's Report Remove can help under-18s seek removal of images posted online. CEOP can help with online sexual abuse, grooming or threats connected with sexual exploitation.
Lesson 2 · Account protection

4. Strong passwords and secure accounts

Passwords protect access to your accounts and the private information inside them. A weak or reused password can help somebody pretend to be you or read things you did not mean to share.

1Three random wordsMake a long, memorable combination of unrelated words.
2One per accountUse separate passwords, especially for your email.
3Extra protectionSwitch on two-step verification where available.
4Keep them privateNever share passwords or one-time codes with others.

✅ Strong approach

Use a made-up example such as VelvetCactusOrbit to understand three random words. Never reuse this example as a real password.

❌ Poor approach

Your name, birth date, pet's name, a common sequence, or the same password everywhere.

If an account might be compromised: tell a trusted adult, change the password using the real service's settings, sign out other sessions where possible, check recovery details and turn on two-step verification.

Never enter your real password into a classroom exercise, a stranger's link or an unfamiliar password-testing website. Use a fictional example for practice.
Lesson 3 · Information you share

5. Personal information and privacy settings

Personal information is information that identifies you, relates to you, or could help someone work out who you are. A harmless-looking detail can become more revealing when combined with others.

🪪 Personal information

Full name, mobile number, school, address, date of birth, location, contact details, photographs and daily routine.

🧩 Information added together

A first name, school badge and geotag might reveal more than each item on its own.

Privacy settings can limit who sees your profile or contacts you. They are useful, but they do not guarantee safety because other people may screenshot, copy or reshare content.

  • Check who can see posts and send messages.
  • Turn off location sharing when you do not need it.
  • Review who can tag you or add you to groups.
  • Ask friends before posting pictures of them.
Lesson 3 · Think before posting

6. Your digital footprint

Digital footprint is the trail of information connected to your online activity. It can include posts, videos, public comments, tagged photos, likes and information collected about your use of services.

🌟 Positive footprint

Showing creativity, helping people, being respectful and sharing achievements appropriately.

🌧️ Negative footprint

Unkind comments, revealing private data, embarrassing a friend or sharing hurtful content.

A post may be saved or passed on after you delete it. Before publishing something, consider who can see it, who it affects and how it might look later.

Lesson 3 · Respectful use

7. Cyberbullying and online behaviour

Cyberbullying is bullying that happens using digital technology. It may involve repeated cruel messages, spreading rumours, fake accounts, humiliating images or deliberately leaving somebody out to upset them.

Be an upstander: Do not join in, like or forward cruel content. Offer support, report harmful behaviour and tell a trusted adult.

Blocking someone can help stop direct contact. Reporting alerts the app or website to behaviour that may break its rules. A school safeguarding adult can help decide what else is needed.

Lesson 3 · Getting the right help

8. Reporting concerns and basic law

SituationFirst route for help
Unkind comments or a fake accountTell a trusted adult; use the app's report/block tools.
Worry about grooming or online sexual abuseTell a trusted adult and report to the CEOP Safety Centre.
Suspected sexual images of children on a websiteTell a trusted adult; report directly to the IWF. Never copy or forward images.
Your own intimate image shared publicly while under 18Childline's Report Remove (in partnership with IWF) and a trusted adult.
Need someone to listenChildline, including free calls to 0800 1111.
Immediate dangerCall 999.

Law challenge: The Computer Misuse Act 1990 covers offences such as accessing computer material without permission. The laws around sexual images of children are more complex: the priority for a pupil who needs help is to seek support, not to try to work out legal consequences alone.

Safety rule: You do not need to investigate or download harmful material to make a report. Explain your concern to a trusted adult and follow the official reporting guidance.
Apply what you know

Five quick scenario checks

Try to explain your answer before opening each example.

1. A group chat starts sharing where everyone lives. What should you think about?

Addresses and routines are personal information. Do not share a home address with people who do not need it; review who is in the group.

2. A friendly player tells you to hide your chats from your family. Why is this worrying?

Requests for secrecy can be a grooming or control warning sign, especially alongside pressure, gifts or sexual comments. Tell a trusted adult.

3. A friend's embarrassing picture is being reposted. How can you help?

Do not forward it. Support your friend and report the post. For intimate images of someone under 18, get adult help and use specialist reporting routes.

4. Somebody knows your game password. What should you do?

Change it, stop using it anywhere else, secure your email and enable two-step verification. Tell an adult if your account was misused.

5. A contact threatens you for refusing to share an intimate image. What happens next?

Do not give in to the threats or send more content. Stop contact and get help straight away from a trusted adult; CEOP is a specialist route for grooming or sexual exploitation.

What should I do first?

1 · Stop and protect yourselfDo not respond to threats or share more personal information. Leave the chat if needed.
2 · Tell someone trustedSpeak to a parent, carer, teacher or school safeguarding adult. You can show the messages safely without forwarding illegal imagery.
